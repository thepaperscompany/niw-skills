# USCIS Policy Manual substance — Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5

Use it when analyzing prongs in depth, when crafting the application strategy, or when the user asks for the legal reasoning behind a rule.

## Section 3a — Prong 1 detail (substantial merit and national importance)

### Substantial merit

The endeavor's merit may be demonstrated in business, entrepreneurship, science, technology, culture, health, or education. USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(3). Merit may be established without immediate or quantifiable economic impact. Endeavors related to research, pure science, and the furtherance of human knowledge may qualify, whether or not the potential accomplishments are likely to translate into economic benefits for the United States. *Matter of Dhanasar*, 26 I&N Dec. 884, 892 (AAO 2016).

### National importance — the analytical lens

The analysis focuses on the prospective impact of the *specific endeavor* the foreign national proposes to undertake. It is **not** about the geographic breadth of the endeavor. National importance is not equivalent to "national in scope." *Matter of Dhanasar*, 26 I&N Dec. at 887, 889. Locally or regionally focused endeavors may have national importance despite being difficult to quantify with respect to geographic scope.

USCIS considers whether the endeavor has:

- National or even global implications within the field, such as certain improved manufacturing processes or medical advances.
- Significant potential to employ U.S. workers, particularly in an economically depressed area, at a level significant with respect to the regional population and economic circumstances.
- Other substantial positive economic effects.
- Broader implications indicating national importance.

*Matter of Dhanasar*, 26 I&N Dec. at 889–90.

### Worked examples from the Policy Manual (use these in user-facing analysis)

These examples are pedagogically useful when explaining why a particular endeavor framing is or is not sufficient. Each is from USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(3).

1. **Classroom teaching example.** A proposed endeavor to engage in classroom teaching, without broader implications for a field or region, **generally does not rise to the level of national importance**. Citing the general importance of the profession of classroom teaching is not alone sufficient. Working in an occupation with a national shortage, or serving as a consultant to others in such an occupation, is also insufficient.

2. **Entrepreneur — general benefits not sufficient.** General assertions about the importance of business owners or entrepreneurs for job creation or economic benefits are not alone sufficient. A petition involving a startup company should demonstrate in detail how the *specific* endeavor meets the national-interest requirement.

3. **Benefits to a specific employer.** Benefits to a specific employer, even one with a national footprint, are not sufficiently relevant to whether the endeavor has national importance. The question is whether the petitioner's own individual endeavor stands to have broader implications — for a field, a region, or the public at large.

4. **Drug-for-pharmaceutical-company example.** A person developing a drug for a pharmaceutical company may establish national importance by demonstrating the prospective public-health benefits of the drug — not by projecting the profits that will accrue to the employer.

5. **Technology-for-employer-clients example.** A person developing a technology for use or sale by a given company may not establish national importance based on evidence that the technology will benefit the company or its clients alone. To establish broader public or commercial implications at a level consistent with national importance, the petitioner could demonstrate widespread interest in adoption or licensing of the technology, a novel and important manufacturing or operational process, or how the technology stands to impact the development of similar technology by other companies.

6. **Software engineer adapting employer's code example.** A software engineer adapting their employer's code for various clients will have difficulty demonstrating the national importance of that endeavor, absent additional broader impacts supported by specific evidence.

These six examples are the operative pattern set adjudicators reach for when evaluating Prong 1. Citing the pattern directly when it applies — by example, not by case name — is a useful discipline.

## Section 3b — Prong 2 detail (well-positioned)

### What USCIS considers

USCIS evaluates, non-exhaustively: education, skills, knowledge, and record of success in related or similar efforts; evidence of a detailed proposal or plan; progress toward achieving the proposed endeavor; and interest or support garnered from potential customers, users, investors, or other relevant entities. *Matter of Dhanasar*, 26 I&N Dec. at 890; USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(3).

Achievements alone, however impressive, do not establish well-positioned status unless they relate to the proposed endeavor.

### Types of evidence

The Policy Manual provides a non-exhaustive list of evidence types. This list is not a checklist; not every item is required, and no single item is sufficient. From USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(3):

- Degrees, certificates, or licenses in the field.
- Patents, trademarks, or copyrights developed by the person.
- Published articles or media reports about the person's achievements or current work.
- Documentation of a strong citation history of the person's work or excerpts of published articles showing positive discourse around, or adoption of, the person's work.
- Evidence that the person's work has influenced the field of endeavor.
- A plan describing how the person intends to continue the proposed work in the United States.
- Correspondence from prospective or potential customers, users, investors, or other relevant entities.
- Documentation reflecting feasible plans for financial support.
- Evidence of investment from U.S. investors such as venture capital firms, angel investors, or accelerators, in amounts appropriate to the endeavor.
- Copies of contracts, agreements, or licenses showing the potential impact of the proposed endeavor.
- Letters from government agencies or quasi-governmental entities demonstrating that the person is well-positioned to advance the endeavor.
- Evidence of awards or grants or other indications of relevant non-monetary support from federal, state, or local government entities with expertise in economic development, research and development, or job creation.
- Evidence demonstrating how the person's work is being used by others (contracts with companies using the person's products, documents showing technology the person invented and how others use it, patents or licenses with documentation of significance to the field).

### The "feasible to succeed" qualifier

A person may be well-positioned to advance an endeavor even if they cannot demonstrate that the endeavor is more likely than not to *ultimately succeed*. *Matter of Dhanasar*, 26 I&N Dec. at 890. However, unsubstantiated claims do not meet the petitioner's burden of proof. The standard is well-positioned, not guaranteed success.

### Letters — the persuasiveness test

Letters from experts may be persuasive when they:

1. Are from experts in the petitioner's field who have first-hand knowledge of the person's achievements.
2. Describe those achievements specifically.
3. Provide specific examples of how the person is well-positioned to advance the endeavor.
4. Are supported by other independent evidence.

USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(3). Letters that praise the petitioner in general terms ("remarkable scholar," "leader in the field") without addressing the specific endeavor's prospective impact are not probative. *Cf. In Re: 37289559* (AAO Mar. 7, 2025) at 3.

## Section 3c — Prong 3 detail (balance of factors)

### The balancing question

Once Prongs 1 and 2 are established, the officer assesses whether the person's endeavor and the person's being well-positioned, taken together, provide benefits to the nation such that a waiver of the labor-certification requirement outweighs the benefits that ordinarily flow from that requirement. *Matter of Dhanasar*, 26 I&N Dec. at 890.

### The three Dhanasar factors

Petitioners may submit evidence relating to one or more of the following:

1. **Impracticality of labor certification.** Whether, in light of the nature of the person's qualifications or proposed endeavor, it would be impractical to obtain a labor certification.
2. **Benefit even if qualified U.S. workers exist.** The benefit to the United States from the person's prospective contributions, even if other U.S. workers were also available.
3. **Urgency.** The national interest in the person's contributions is sufficiently urgent to warrant forgoing the labor-certification process — such as a time-sensitive public-health or safety benefit.

### More specific considerations from the Policy Manual

- Whether the labor-certification process may prevent an employer from hiring a person with unique knowledge or skills exceeding the minimum standard for that occupation, which cannot be appropriately captured by labor certification.
- Whether the endeavor has the potential to generate considerable economic impact (consistent, for example, with economic revitalization).
- Whether the endeavor may lead to potential job creation.

### What Prong 3 does NOT support

Evidence of a national labor shortage in the person's occupation, by itself, does not satisfy Prong 3. USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(3). Nor does a generic statement that the U.S. would benefit from the person's work. Prong 3 needs *its own* substance — not a restatement of Prong 1 in different words.

## Section 4 — STEM and critical-and-emerging-technologies considerations

USCIS recognizes the role of persons with advanced STEM degrees in fostering progress in critical and emerging technologies and other STEM areas important to U.S. competitiveness or national security. USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(4).

### Identifying a critical and emerging technology field

Officers consider governmental, academic, and other authoritative sources. The most-cited authority is the National Science and Technology Council's *Critical and Emerging Technologies List Update*, published by the Executive Office of the President. Other authoritative sources include the President's annual research-and-development priorities memo (issued jointly by OSTP and OMB) and the Interim National Security Strategic Guidance.

### The "strong positive factor" triad (for Prong 3)

USCIS treats the following combination of facts as a strong positive factor in Prong 3:

1. The person possesses an advanced STEM degree, especially a Ph.D.
2. The person will be engaged in work furthering a critical and emerging technology, or other STEM area important to U.S. competitiveness.
3. The person is well-positioned to advance the proposed STEM endeavor of national importance.

**All three legs must be present** for the triad to apply. The benefit is especially weighty where the endeavor has the potential to support U.S. national security or enhance U.S. economic competitiveness, or when the petition is supported by letters from interested U.S. government agencies.

## Section 5 — Interested government agency and quasi-governmental entity letters

The Policy Manual treats letters from interested U.S. government agencies or quasi-governmental entities as a particular evidentiary category. USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(5).

### Who counts

- Federal agencies (NIH, DOE, NSF, DARPA, DOD, ARPA-H, CDC, etc.).
- Federally funded research and development centers (e.g., national labs).
- State and local government economic-development authorities.
- Quasi-governmental entities (e.g., certain federally chartered research consortia).

### What a strong letter looks like

A letter is persuasive when it:

1. Is on official agency letterhead, signed by an identifiable official with relevant authority.
2. Describes how the petitioner's specific work advances the agency's mission or a documented federal priority.
3. Identifies specific examples of the petitioner's contribution — methods, deployments, programs, dataset releases — that the agency has used or values.
4. Does not simply praise the field generally.

A letter that says only "X is an important field and we welcome talented researchers in it" provides minimal value. A letter that says "Dr. Y's mobility-data toolkit has been adopted by our agency's transit-equity program at three named transit agencies and is informing our 2027 grant-program design" provides material Prong 2 evidence and supports Prong 3 urgency.

### Important caveat

An interested-government-agency letter is **not** required for an NIW. The absence of such a letter is not, by itself, a reason to dismiss. The Policy Manual lists this category alongside many others. Do not over-promise the user that securing such a letter will be approval-determinative.

## Section 6 — Specific evidentiary considerations for entrepreneurs

USCIS recognizes that entrepreneur petitions have unique evidentiary considerations. USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(6).

### The threshold rule for entrepreneurs

Not every entrepreneur qualifies. Broad assertions about the importance of the entity's industry or about general job-creation potential will not establish NIW eligibility. The entity-specific case must be made.

**Examples of insufficient framing:**

- "I am opening a consulting firm for those working in a nationally important occupation." Not sufficient on its own.
- "The car-dealership industry is important." Not sufficient.
- "Our company is in a $14-billion market." Not sufficient. Market size is not endeavor importance.

The Policy Manual is explicit that statements about industry size or general entrepreneur benefits are not adequate.

### Evidence categories the Policy Manual recognizes for entrepreneurs

**Ownership and role in the U.S.-based entity.**

- Ownership interest in the entity, including founder or co-founder status.
- Active and central role — typically as an officer or in another key position — such that the petitioner's knowledge, skills, or experience would significantly advance the proposed endeavor.

This is Prong 2 evidence (well-positioned), not Prong 1. Ownership alone rarely establishes eligibility.

**Investments.**

- Investment, binding commitment to invest, or other evidence demonstrating future intent to invest from outside investors (angel investors, venture capital firms, accelerators) at amounts consistent with industry standards for the relevant endeavor.
- The Policy Manual recognizes that different endeavors have different capital needs. A $500K seed round for a software company is meaningful; the same amount for a biotech may be insufficient. Evaluate against industry norms.

Investment is also Prong 2 evidence. It validates that experienced outside parties have assessed the endeavor and committed resources.

**Revenue generation, growth in revenue, and job creation.**

- Growth metrics — revenue generated, year-over-year growth, jobs created in the United States.
- The petitioner's contribution to those metrics (especially as founder, CTO, or similar key role).

Revenue and job creation can support Prong 1 (national importance) **only when combined with additional context** — for example, that the entity is in an economically depressed area, or that the job creation is significant relative to the regional population. Generic growth claims do not establish national importance.

### What strong entrepreneur petitions typically demonstrate

Looking across approved entrepreneur petitions discussed in public AAO decisions and adjudication patterns:

- Documented traction with named users, customers, or pilot partners (not aspirational).
- Investor commitments from named funds or angels with specific dollar amounts.
- Specific operational metrics (revenue, employee count, deployment scale) attributable to the petitioner's leadership.
- Evidence of broader-than-employer impact — adoption by other companies, standards-body participation, policy or industry references tied specifically to the entity's work.

### What the Policy Manual specifically de-emphasizes

- Industry size or market size, used as a Prong 1 hook.
- Generic job-creation potential, used without specific traction.
- Pre-product, pre-revenue ventures relying primarily on the founder's prior credentials.

For pre-product founders, the most credible NIW path is to wait until concrete entity-specific progress is documented (pilot signed, prototype deployed, customer LOIs converted to paid contracts, regulatory submission filed). Filing too early often produces denials that complicate later strategy.
