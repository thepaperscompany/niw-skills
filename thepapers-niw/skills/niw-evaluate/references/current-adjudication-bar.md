# Current adjudication bar — operational standards

These are the operational standards that reflect how USCIS adjudicators and the Administrative Appeals Office (AAO) are actually deciding NIW cases under the current USCIS Policy Manual guidance. These are not additional legal rules — they are the lens through which the *Matter of Dhanasar* framework must be applied today.

Missing these patterns is the single biggest reason recently filed petitions are dismissed on appeal. Read this file before drafting any analysis.

## 1. Endeavor-specificity is dispositive (Prong 1)

- Prong 1 asks about the **endeavor**, not the **field**. Evidence about the importance of a field (Big Data projected to contribute $X trillion; AI on the Critical and Emerging Technologies List; public transportation is important; climate change is urgent) is **insufficient on its own**. Such field-level evidence is necessary framing but cannot substitute for documentary evidence of the *specific* endeavor's prospective impact. *See Matter of Dhanasar*, 26 I&N Dec. at 889; *In Re: 37289559*, March 7, 2025, at 4.
- Check whether the proposed endeavor is described narrowly enough: what specific projects, products, methods, deployments, or interventions will the person produce? An endeavor framed at the level of a theme (e.g., "leveraging human mobility data to develop fair and interpretable models for equitable urban environments") reads as a research area, not an endeavor, and is a major risk flag.
- A qualifying endeavor names concrete outputs, intended users or beneficiaries, and identifiable impact pathways.
- Citations, publications, funding received, and field-level policy references do **not** establish national importance on their own. The question is whether the record shows the **specific** endeavor will have broader-than-employer, broader-than-project implications.

## 2. Well-credentialed ≠ well-positioned (Prong 2)

- A degree, even a Ph.D. from a top institution, is explicitly insufficient per USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(4). Prong 2 requires affirmative evidence of **record of success** in matters related to the specific endeavor, including evidence that others are using or adopting the person's work, progress already achieved toward the endeavor, and support/interest from relevant entities.
- Scrutinize **citation data for independence**. Raw citation counts without a breakdown of independent vs. self-citation or collaborator citation are weak. *See In Re: 37289559* at 4 ("The Petitioner did not specify how many of the citations were by independent researchers"). AAO decisions routinely call this out.
- Scrutinize **role on funded research or team projects**. Being listed on an NSF/NIH grant is not the same as being PI or Co-PI. *In Re: 37289559* at 4. A contributor to an important project is not automatically well-positioned to advance their own endeavor.
- Scrutinize **recommender independence**. Letters from academic advisors, co-authors, or direct collaborators carry less weight than letters from independent experts with first-hand knowledge of the person's work through reading, adoption, or citation.
- A strong Prong 2 record typically shows evidence in **at least three** of these categories: independent citation/adoption, tangible progress, third-party interest (investors, customers, agencies), and independent expert endorsement. Two or fewer is a risk flag.

## 3. Prong 1 and Prong 2 must remain separate

- Prong 1 is about the **endeavor**; Prong 2 is about the **person**. Using the petitioner's achievements, publications, or credentials as Prong 1 evidence is a category error that adjudicators penalize.
- When analyzing Prong 1, draw only on evidence about the endeavor itself — what it is, why it matters beyond the employer or project, who will use or benefit from it.
- When analyzing Prong 2, draw only on evidence about the person — what they have done, what others have said about their work, what progress they have made.

## 4. Prong 3 requires a structured balancing analysis, not a conclusory statement

- Evaluate each Dhanasar Prong 3 factor explicitly:
  - **Impracticality of labor certification** given the nature of the endeavor.
  - **Benefit to the U.S. even if qualified U.S. workers exist** — name the specific unique value.
  - **Urgency / time-sensitivity** (public health, national security, critical emerging tech).
- For STEM petitioners, evaluate the "strong positive factor" triad from USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(4): advanced STEM degree (especially Ph.D.) + work in a critical and emerging technology or area important to U.S. competitiveness/national security + well-positioned. **All three legs must be present** for the triad to apply.
- A national labor shortage in the field is **not sufficient** for Prong 3.
- A Prong 3 conclusion reached by restating Prong 1 in different words is not sufficient. Call this out if you see it.

## 5. Red-flag patterns from recent AAO dismissals

Treat the presence of any of the following as significant denial risk and call it out explicitly:

a. Endeavor described at the theme or research-area level, not the specific project level.
b. Evidence of national importance consists primarily of articles, reports, or government lists about the **field** rather than the person's specific work.
c. Support letters describe the person as "remarkable," "respected," or "a leader in the field" without addressing the specific endeavor's prospective impact. *Cf. In Re: 37289559* at 3.
d. Publications with modest citation counts (e.g., under ~30) and no breakdown showing independent citations.
e. No evidence of third-party adoption, licensing, implementation, deployment, or use by others.
f. For industry professionals: work framed as benefiting the employer or its clients, without broader-implications evidence.
g. For academic researchers: no evidence that research has influenced policy, practice, or other researchers' work beyond citation counts.
h. For entrepreneurs: claims based on industry/market size or general job-creation assertions, without endeavor-specific plans, traction, and commitments. USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 6 Pt. F Ch. 5(D)(6).
i. Endeavor only tenuously connected to the person's documented expertise (risks both Prong 1 and Prong 2).
j. Reliance on the Critical and Emerging Technologies List without showing the person's specific work advances that technology.

## 6. Calibration — the current bar is high

Recent USCIS Policy Manual updates, recent AAO dismissals, and widely reported RFE trends all indicate that adjudication is stricter than it used to be. A profile that would have been approvable several years ago is not necessarily approvable today. When in doubt, your assessment should reflect this reality rather than default to encouraging language. You are serving the petitioner by giving them an honest evaluation of their risk — not by telling them what they hope to hear. A case that an AAO officer could reasonably dismiss on Prong 1 specificity, Prong 2 documentation, or unsupported assertions is a High Risk case even if the underlying credentials are impressive.

## 7. Never invent evidence

If the profile does not document a claim, do not assert that claim as a strength. An unsupported claim is a weakness, not a strength. Say so explicitly and identify the evidence the petitioner would need to gather. See the anti-hallucination rules in `SKILL.md`.

## 8. Industry professionals vs. academic researchers

The Dhanasar standard is the same, but what qualifies as evidence differs.

- **Academic researchers:** independent citations with breakdown, peer invitations (reviewing, editorial boards), adoption of methods/datasets/code by other research groups, mentions in policy documents that tie specifically to the person's work, follow-on work building on the person's findings.
- **Industry professionals:** product/technology adoption (licensing, enterprise deployments, market share), patents being licensed or cited by other inventors, media coverage or industry analyst mentions tying specifically to the person's work, standards-body participation, named customer references.
- Do not hold industry professionals to academic evidence types — but do require **equivalent** evidence of broader adoption and third-party validation. The bar is not publications; the bar is documented prospective impact beyond the employer.
