# Recommendation and expert letters

Letters are advisory, never presumptive evidence, and are weighed on what the writer could actually know. *Matter of Caron International*, 19 I&N Dec. 791 (Comm'r 1988). Handled well they carry real weight; handled badly they are the fastest way to lose credibility across the whole record.

## Assigning writers to what they can prove

Start from the gaps, not from the list of people willing to write. For each writer ask: what single thing does this person have first-hand knowledge of that no one else on the list does?

- Someone who **adopted, cited, funded, licensed or built on** the work speaks to Prong 2 positioning, and is usually the most valuable letter available.
- A **direct supervisor or advisor** can attest to specific contributions and role, which is worth having, but carries less weight.
- A **senior figure in the field with no relationship** can speak to the endeavor's significance, but only if they engage the endeavor specifically rather than praising the person.

## Independence

Discount, in this order: the petitioner's own statement, then direct supervisors and close collaborators or co-authors, then independent experts with first-hand knowledge through reading, adopting, citing or funding the work.

Where a writer is genuinely independent, make that visible in the letter: how they came to know the work, and that they have no employment, funding or authorship relationship with the petitioner.

## No two letters covering the same ground

Overlapping letters do not add weight. They subtract it, because a set of letters that all say the same thing reads as coordinated rather than corroborative.

Assign each writer a distinct assignment before drafting anything, and keep a visible map of who covers what. If two writers can only speak to the same thing, use the more independent one and ask the other for something different or not at all.

## Write only what this writer could know

**A letter asserting knowledge the writer could not have is the single most common reason a letter is discounted entirely, and the discount often spreads to the rest.**

A complete profile is context for you, not a script. An academic advisor knows the research and publications. An industry colleague knows the project work. An independent expert can speak to field-wide impact and to how they encountered the work. None of them knows all of it.

Three things a writer genuinely knows beats a recitation of the CV.

## What makes a letter carry weight

- It describes the **proposed endeavor** and its prospective impact, not only the petitioner's past.
- It gives **concrete, personal detail**: what specifically changed in the writer's own work or organization because of the petitioner's contribution.
- It is **corroborated** by documents elsewhere in the record.

Letters lose weight when they repeat what is already in the record, praise qualifications without addressing the endeavor, describe benefits to clients rather than to the field, or rest on a business plan the record does not support, since the plan's weakness cascades into every opinion built on it.

## Integrity

A letter must be genuinely authored or genuinely adopted by the person who signs it. A signature creates a strong presumption of knowledge and assent, and a record found to rest on letters the named authors did not actually write faces consequences far beyond a refusal, reaching the petitioner's admissibility.

Draft letters are drafts. Every writer must review, edit and adopt their own letter as their own words before it is signed. Say this plainly whenever you produce one.
