# What changes once the petition is filed

Filing freezes two things: the facts that can establish eligibility, and the endeavor the case is about. Almost every intuitive response to a weak filed record violates one of them.

## Rule 1: eligibility is fixed as of the filing date

A petitioner must establish eligibility at the time of filing and continue to be eligible through adjudication. 8 CFR 103.2(b)(1), 8 CFR 103.2(b)(12); *Matter of Katigbak*, 14 I&N Dec. 45 (Reg. Comm'r 1971).

A fact that came into being after filing cannot establish eligibility, however favorable it is. This forecloses the most intuitive response to a weak record:

- refreshed citation counts or an updated bibliometric export,
- papers published since filing,
- a promotion, a new role, or a new title,
- newly awarded funding or a new grant,
- a new deployment, customer, or adoption.

Adjudicators reject these as presenting a set of facts that did not exist at the time of filing. Submitting them does not merely fail; it signals that the record as filed did not carry the burden.

## Rule 2: the proposed endeavor is locked by the petition as filed

Restating, re-scoping, broadening, narrowing, sharpening, or replacing the proposed endeavor to fit a notice is an impermissible material change. *Matter of Izummi*, 22 I&N Dec. 169 (Assoc. Comm'r 1998); *Matter of Katigbak*. Adjudicators analyze the case on the endeavor as originally described, and hold the change against the petitioner's consistency.

**This binds even when the notice itself asks for "a detailed description of the proposed endeavor," or says the endeavor is not specific enough.** That request asks for detail and evidence about the endeavor already described: what it concretely consists of, who it reaches, what its prospective impact is, and which documents establish that. It is not an invitation to describe a different or more impressive endeavor.

Say this to the petitioner explicitly whenever a notice raises endeavor specificity, because the intuitive response is the one that loses the case.

## Rule 3: answer the notice, and only the notice

A response is a single submission covering every requested item. Submitting only part of what was requested is treated as a request for a decision on the record as it stands. 8 CFR 103.2(b)(11).

Everything in that one submission competes for one adjudicator's attention. Service center notices routinely resolve elements in the petitioner's favor while contesting others. Do not argue an element the notice expressly conceded, and do not volunteer evidence on it. If the record looks weak on a conceded element, that is not this document's problem to solve.

## What you may still do

The distinction that makes a response possible: **evidence created now may document a fact that already existed on the filing date.**

- a letter written today describing a role held before filing,
- a citation or adoption report scoped and dated as of the filing date,
- records of pre-filing usage that were never submitted,
- an explanation, with documents, of what the filed exhibits already establish.

Phrase every evidentiary suggestion so the filing-date scope is visible on its face: "scoped and dated as of the filing date," "describing the role as held before filing."

## Deadlines: never compute one

**Read the deadline printed on the notice. Never calculate it.**

Do not add 84 days, 87 days, 12 weeks, or any other period to the notice date, and never fall back to a typical or maximum period when no date is printed. An officer may set a period shorter than the maximum, so a computed date can fall *after* the real one, and a petitioner who relies on it misses a deadline that regulations do not permit extending.

Related discipline: the service method is unstated unless the notice itself says how it was served. A scanned PDF is not evidence of mail service, and an uploaded file is not evidence of online-account service. Where the notice does not state something, report it as blank. An honest blank is useful; a guess is not.

## Why these rules are counterintuitive

Every one of them tells a petitioner not to do the thing that feels most helpful: not to send the better citation count, not to sharpen the endeavor description the officer just called vague, not to shore up the weak part the notice did not ask about. A tool that reasons from general knowledge about "responding to feedback" will recommend all three. That is why these rules are written down.
