# Reading a record: basis, tier, filing status, independence

Four questions must be answered separately about every material claim in a case. Collapsing any two of them into a single rating is the most common way a review becomes useless, because the cure for each is different.

## The four questions

| Question | Asks | Values |
|---|---|---|
| **Basis** | Does something in the package actually prove this? | `Documented`, `Inferred`, `Unsupported` |
| **Tier** | What form does that proof take? | `primary`, `secondary`, `affidavit`, `not_documented` |
| **Filing status** | Has USCIS seen it? | `filed`, `unconfirmed`, `not_filed` |
| **Independence** | Is the person vouching at arm's length? | `independent`, `collaborator`, `supervisor`, `self` |

A claim can be Documented, primary, and still weak because the only person attesting to it is the petitioner's own co-author. A claim can be strongly independent and still fail because the document was never filed. Answer all four.

## Question 1: basis (is it documented?)

- **`Documented`**: an exhibit, the petition draft, or a recommendation letter actually substantiates the claim. When an exhibit substantiates it, name the exhibit id. When the petition draft or a letter carries it instead, say which, because neither is an exhibit.
- **`Inferred`**: plausible from context, but nothing in the package proves it.
- **`Unsupported`**: asserted with nothing behind it.

**Only `Documented` evidence carries the preponderance burden.** Inferred and Unsupported material can shade a judgment. It cannot establish a prong. This is the verdict floor, and it is what stops an impressive-looking profile from producing a strong verdict on an undocumented record.

## Question 2: evidence tier under 8 CFR 103.2(b)(2)

The regulation sets an order of preference and makes the lower tiers conditional:

- **`primary`**: the document itself. The degree certificate, the patent grant, the executed contract, the published paper.
- **`secondary`**: acceptable only on a showing that primary evidence is unavailable.
- **`affidavit`**: acceptable only on a showing that both primary and secondary evidence are unavailable.
- **`not_documented`**: nothing in the package carries the claim.

**Petitioners reach for affidavits first, because they are the easiest to obtain.** That is exactly the order the regulation forbids. A claim resting on an affidavit where primary evidence plainly exists is a gap worth naming even when the affidavit is credible, and the cure is usually easy: ask for the document.

When you record a lower tier, say what showing of unavailability exists, or note that none does.

## Question 3: filing status (has USCIS actually seen it?)

Every exhibit in a manifest carries one of three values, and the value changes what may be said about it:

- **`filed`** (`FILED WITH THE PETITION`), USCIS has this. Cite it freely.
- **`unconfirmed`** (`FILING STATUS UNCONFIRMED`), the ordinary case for a package still being assembled. Treat as citable, and say the status is unconfirmed when it matters.
- **`not_filed`** (`NOT FILED`), the petitioner holds this document but never sent it. **USCIS has not seen it. Never cite it as being in the record, and never let it move an assessment toward "already sufficient."**

A `not_filed` document is the best kind of gap to find, because the petitioner already has it and only has to submit it. Say that plainly: they are producing a copy, not going to find one.

The failure this prevents is specific and silent. A review that counts held-but-unfiled documents as record support tells a petitioner their record is stronger than the one USCIS will read.

## Question 4: independence (who is vouching?)

Letters are advisory, never presumptive evidence, and are weighed on what the writer could actually know. *Matter of Caron International*, 19 I&N Dec. 791 (Comm'r 1988).

Discount, in roughly this order:
- **`self`**: the petitioner's own statement, uncorroborated.
- **`supervisor`** and **`collaborator`**: a direct supervisor, advisor, or co-author. Real knowledge, reduced weight.
- **`independent`**: an expert with first-hand knowledge of the work through reading, adopting, citing, or funding it, who is not a collaborator.

Letters lose weight when they repeat what is already in the record, praise qualifications without addressing the specific endeavor, describe benefits to clients rather than to the field, or rest on an unreliable business plan, since the plan's unreliability cascades into every opinion built on it.

Letters are not inherently weak. Records that succeed rest heavily on letters that are specific, personalized, focused on the endeavor rather than the person, and corroborated by documents.

## Worked example

> "The petitioner's fraud-detection system processes 2 million transactions daily."

- **Basis**: `Documented` if an exhibit shows it; `Inferred` if only a letter asserts it in passing; `Unsupported` if only the petition says so.
- **Tier**: `primary` for a system architecture document or an employer's operational report; `affidavit` if the only support is a colleague's declaration.
- **Filing status**: if that exhibit is `not_filed`, the claim is not in the record USCIS reads, whatever its tier.
- **Independence**: an employer letter is `supervisor`. A customer confirming they run the system is `independent` and worth far more.

Four answers, four different cures.

## The rule that ties them together

Provenance, tier, and sufficiency are different questions and must never be collapsed into one rating. A review that reports "strong evidence" without saying which of the four it means gives the petitioner nothing to act on. State each, then say what the combination establishes and where it stops.
