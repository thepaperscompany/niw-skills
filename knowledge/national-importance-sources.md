# Evidence that an endeavor advances a national priority

## Why field-level evidence fails

Prong 1 asks about the petitioner's **specific proposed endeavor**, not the field it sits in. Evidence that artificial intelligence is a critical and emerging technology, that a market is projected to reach some figure, or that a public problem is serious, is framing. It does not establish that this endeavor has national importance, and records resting on it are refused for exactly that reason.

The question a source has to help answer is narrower: is there a named national priority that **this endeavor's specific outputs** would advance, and is that advance plausible from what the endeavor actually is?

## The three tiers

**Tier A, endeavor-specific.** U.S. government programs, funding opportunities, contracts, congressional or GAO reports, agency strategic plans, or regulations that target the **specific** problem, technology, method or outcome the endeavor addresses. A named agency program matching the endeavor's technology class is Tier A.

**Tier B, sub-field priority.** National strategies naming the endeavor's specific sub-area rather than the broad field.

**Tier C, field-level framing.** White House fact sheets, general agency mission statements, technology lists, market reports. Useful as opening context. **It cannot carry Prong 1 on its own, and must be labelled as context rather than dressed up as proof.**

For each source, record: its full official title, the issuing body, the tier with one phrase on why, the concrete connection between the source and the endeavor's specific **outputs** (and if the honest connection is only field-level, say so), how it should be used, and a short quotation copied exactly from the source text.

## Rating what you found, honestly

- **Strong**: two or more Tier A sources.
- **Moderate**: one Tier A, or two or more Tier B.
- **Weak**: only Tier C.

If the rating is not Strong, say what to do about it, and do not suggest loading up on more Tier C framing. The useful next step is usually to seek letters from people at the specific programs named, or to document adoption directly.

**If no Tier A or B source genuinely fits, say so plainly. Do not invent one, and do not promote a Tier C source to make the rating look better.** A petition citing fewer real sources is far stronger than one citing an authority that does not say what it was claimed to say.

## Rules that keep this from producing fabrications

1. **Search. Do not answer from memory.** Program names, report titles and agency initiatives change, and a plausible-sounding one that does not exist is worse than nothing.
2. **Never write a URL you did not retrieve.** A link from memory is dead or wrong.
3. **Never place text in quotation marks unless it was copied from the source you actually read.** Where no verbatim quotation is available, say so rather than composing one.
4. **Do not invent report numbers, docket numbers, order numbers or program names.**
5. **Prefer official U.S. government sources**: the White House, executive agencies, Congress, GAO, national laboratories, agency strategic plans and funding announcements. News articles and academic papers are not primary evidence of a national priority.
