# Reading a USCIS notice: what to transcribe, and what never to compute

A Request for Evidence or Notice of Intent to Deny puts the petitioner on a clock they cannot reset. The reading tasks below are mechanical, and getting one wrong costs the case in a way no argument can repair.

## The deadline: read it, never compute it

**Transcribe the response deadline exactly as printed on the notice.**

Do not add 84 days, 87 days, 12 weeks, or any other period to the notice date. Do not fall back to a typical or maximum period when no date is printed. Do not reason from what the maximum allows.

The reason is specific. An officer may set a response period **shorter** than the maximum, so a computed date can fall *after* the real one. A petitioner who relies on a computed date can miss a deadline that regulations do not permit extending, and the petition is then decided on the record as it stands.

If the notice prints no deadline, say so plainly and tell the petitioner to find it on the notice or in their USCIS online account. An honest blank is useful. A guess is not.

The maximum response period for an RFE is 84 days from the date of the notice, and USCIS does not grant extensions. Know this so you recognize an unusually short period when you see one, and never as a substitute for reading the printed date.

## Service method: unstated unless stated

Record the service method as **not stated** unless the notice itself says how it was served.

A scanned PDF is not evidence of mail service. A file the petitioner uploaded is not evidence of online-account service. Mailing adds days to a response period in a way that electronic service does not, so inferring the method silently changes the deadline arithmetic in the petitioner's head.

## What else to transcribe, character for character

- **Notice date**, as the notice bears it.
- **Receipt number**, character for character.
- **Every quoted deficiency**, copied exactly. Do not insert, drop, or substitute a single word, including one that seems obviously implied. Adding "specific" to "the petitioner's proposed endeavor" changes what the notice says on the very point it is contesting.
- Do not normalize a phrase to wording the notice uses elsewhere. Copy the wording at the place you are quoting.
- If a passage is too long to reproduce exactly, quote a shorter span you can reproduce exactly, or elide with "..." between spans you copy exactly.

Dates go in as `YYYY-MM-DD`. A notice printing "October 24, 2026" is recorded as `2026-10-24`.

## RFE and NOID are not the same notice

- A **Request for Evidence** says the record as filed does not establish eligibility and asks for more.
- A **Notice of Intent to Deny** says USCIS has reached a preliminary adverse conclusion and is giving the petitioner an opportunity to respond before denying. A NOID is the more serious posture and often reflects a concern about the credibility or sufficiency of what was already submitted, not merely its absence.

Identify which one the document is, and say so. The response strategy differs: a NOID has to engage the reasoning behind the preliminary conclusion, not simply supply documents.

## One submission, covering everything requested

A response is a single submission covering every requested item. Submitting only part of what was requested is treated as a request for a decision on the record as it stands. 8 CFR 103.2(b)(11).

Two consequences:

1. **Everything competes for one adjudicator's attention.** Volume spent on a settled question displaces the argument that was actually requested.
2. **There is no second round.** Plan the response as the only one.

## What the notice concedes

Service center notices routinely resolve elements in the petitioner's favor while contesting others, for example stating that the petitioner meets the second prong. Record every conceded element explicitly.

Do not raise a deficiency, and do not submit evidence, on an element the notice has expressly conceded. If the record looks weak there, that is not this document's problem to solve. Arguing a conceded point invites the adjudicator to reconsider it.
